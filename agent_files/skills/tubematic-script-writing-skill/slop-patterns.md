# Slop patterns: what makes narration sound machine-written

Read this once before you draft the three openings. It lists the habits that make a script sound
like a language model wrote it, and says what to write instead. It covers the words a listener
hears: each segment's `voice`, the three openings, the publish title and the description. It does
not cover `visual` briefs.

A model writes whatever is most likely to come next, so each habit below is the safe choice that
fits any topic. A good script makes the choice that fits only this one. The patterns are listed
strongest first. The first six justify a rewrite on a single sighting. The later ones need company:
act on them when two or more sit in the same passage.

**Rules that apply to every pattern.**

- A rewrite keeps every number, name, date, quote and claim. Slop removal changes how a fact is
  said, never which facts are there. If a sentence needs a detail you do not have, write a plainer
  sentence. Never invent one.
- Every sentence you keep adds a fact the viewer does not have yet.
- Fix the paragraph, not the phrase. Swapping one stock word for another leaves the habit in place.
- A pattern stays when it is the point. Each entry says when.
- The script's profile and the visual style set the voice. Neither licenses a pattern below.

**Already covered elsewhere, not repeated here.** Greetings and agendas, staged run-ups ("Here's
the thing"), recaps, closing asks, em dashes, a closing line after the payoff: the Step 4 grammar
and the vocabulary rules in `SKILL.md` own those.

## Staging: the sentence announces instead of saying

### 1. Not X but Y

**Watch for:** "not just X, but Y"; "it's not X, it's Y"; "this isn't about X. It's about Y."; "this
doesn't mean X. It means Y."; "a feature, not a bug"; a clipped tail ("no guessing").

**Why it fails:** the denied half names something nobody claimed, so the real half sounds bigger
without being any bigger. The viewer hears a pose.

**Before:** The screen isn't feeling pressure at all. It's sensing you.
**After:** The screen senses the charge your finger draws away, so pressing harder changes nothing.

**Keep it when** the denied half is something this viewer believes and the formula's turn is to
correct it ("is the tool broken?" answered with "the tool is fine"). Say the belief first so the
correction answers it. A denial the viewer never held is the tell.

### 2. One-line closers and drumrolls

**Watch for:** a short sentence that repeats the one before ("That changes everything."); "Let that
sink in."; "Read that again."; a row of fragments ("No map. No plan. No way back."); a sentence
after an example that names what the example showed.

**Why it fails:** the line asks the viewer to pause on a claim and adds none. A listener cannot
skim past it the way a reader can, so each one costs real seconds.

**Before:** The ship had no map. No plan. No way back.
**After:** The ship left with no chart past the second island and no fuel to return.

**Keep it when** the short sentence carries a new fact, such as the turn sentence of three to ten
words that Step 4 asks for. A drumroll of three or more is the tell.

### 3. Sayings that sound deep

**Watch for:** "at its core"; "the real question is"; "what really matters"; "the heart of the
matter"; "the language of trust"; "the currency of attention"; "X becomes a trap".

**Why it fails:** an ordinary point is dressed as a hidden truth, and the dressing adds no detail.

**Before:** At its core, the real question is whether the log can be trusted.
**After:** The log is only as good as the guess that pairs a deleted name with an added one.

### 4. Arguing with no one

**Watch for:** "I'm not saying"; "don't get me wrong"; "you might think... but"; "to be clear"; "some
would say"; "it would be easy to assume".

**Why it fails:** it invents an objector so the next sentence can win. Voicing the viewer's real
objection is fine (grammar rule 7). Inventing a vague opponent is not.

**Before:** You might think the cache is the problem. But it isn't.
**After:** The cache returns the right answer in under a millisecond. The slow part is the lock.

### 5. Rhetorical-question setups

**Watch for:** a question asked only to answer it a sentence later ("So what does that mean? It
means..."); two or three questions in a row.

**Why it fails:** the question carries no information the answer does not.

**Keep it when** it is the one question the whole script answers (grammar rules 3 and 7).

### 6. Narrated candor

**Watch for:** "Honestly?"; "Look,"; "Real talk:"; "Let's be honest"; "I'll be straight with you".

**Why it fails:** it claims frankness the next sentence has to earn. Say the frank thing.

## Rhythm: the pattern was applied by rule

### 7. Forced triads

**Watch for:** three parallel items where two facts exist ("fast, cheap, and reliable"); "X, Y, and
Z" in every paragraph; a colon that leads into three items.

**Before:** The tool is fast, simple, and secure.
**After:** The tool checks each file once, and the check takes about a second.

**Keep it when** there are exactly three real things.

### 8. Same-opener runs

**Watch for:** three or more sentences in a row that open with the same word ("It... It... It...",
"This... This...").

**Fix:** join two, or lead one with the thing that changed.

### 9. Metronome length

**Watch for:** five sentences of about the same length in a row, or every paragraph ending on a
short punch.

**Fix:** vary it on purpose. One long sentence that carries a mechanism, then one short turn.

### 10. Stacked hedges

**Watch for:** "could potentially", "might arguably", "in some cases it may", "it's possible that
perhaps".

**Why it fails:** the payoff must be a definite answer (grammar rule 9). One honest hedge on a
fact that is uncertain stays. A stack never does.

## Inflation: ordinary facts dressed as important

### 11. Inflated significance

**Watch for:** "stands as a testament"; "plays a pivotal role"; "a game-changer"; "reshaped the
landscape"; "an enduring legacy"; "marks a turning point".

**Before:** The cable stands as a testament to Victorian ambition.
**After:** The cable carried 732 messages before the insulation failed, and the first one took
sixteen hours to send.

Replace the claim of importance with the fact that makes it important.

### 12. False agency

**Watch for:** an idea or object doing a human verb: "the decision emerges"; "the market
punishes"; "the data tells us"; "the complaint becomes a fix".

**Fix:** name who did it ("the engineer cut the fuel line"; "traders sold").

### 13. Avoiding "is" and "has"

**Watch for:** "serves as", "stands as", "functions as", "boasts", "features", "marks".

**Fix:** write "is" or "has".

### 14. Shallow -ing tails

**Watch for:** a sentence that ends with "highlighting...", "underscoring...", "ensuring...",
"showcasing...", "reflecting...".

**Fix:** cut the tail, or make it its own sentence with a fact in it.

### 15. Borrowed authority and vague attribution

**Watch for:** "experts say"; "studies show"; "researchers believe"; "many people think" with no
name behind them.

**Fix:** name the person, the paper or the year, or cut the claim (grammar rule 14).

### 16. "Real" and "actual" inflation

**Watch for:** "the real reason", "the actual problem", "what's really going on".

**Fix:** state the reason. Keep "actually" only where it corrects a stated belief.

### 17. Sales language

**Watch for:** "stunning", "breathtaking", "groundbreaking", "rich tapestry", "diverse array",
"nestled", "must-see".

## Leftovers from a chat

### 18. Chatbot residue

**Watch for:** "Great question"; "Certainly"; "I hope this helps"; "As an AI"; "Let me know if".
None of it belongs in a script, and none is on this list by accident: it leaks in when a draft is
pasted back.

### 19. Knowledge-limit and source talk

**Watch for:** "as of my last update"; "based on available information"; "details are limited".
Say what is known and what is not, in one plain sentence, or leave it out.

## Stock words

Drop these on sight. They carry no fact and a listener hears them as filler.

| Avoid | Write |
|---|---|
| delve into, dive into | look at, open |
| tapestry, landscape (figurative) | the thing itself |
| game-changer, groundbreaking | what changed, in a number |
| unlock the power / secret / potential | what you can now do |
| in today's fast-paced world | cut it |
| leverage, utilize | use |
| navigate (a problem) | handle, fix |
| crucial, pivotal, vital | say what breaks without it |
| realm, testament, beacon | the plain noun |

## What the server checks

`update_video` returns advisory warnings for the patterns that can be found without judgment:
`contrast_staging` (pattern 1), `stock_phrase` (patterns 3 and 11 and the stock words),
`fragment_row` (pattern 2) and the wider `staged_runup`. They are advice and never refuse a script.
The rest of this file has no check, because it takes a reader to tell a habit from a point. Read
each warning against the script: fix the ones that name a real habit, and leave the ones that
catch a contrast the viewer holds, with a reason in your hand-off comment.
